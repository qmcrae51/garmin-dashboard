GYM

Gym A · Monday · Strength

Heavy, low reps, full rest (2–3 min). Strength without bulk.

  • Trap-bar deadlift or back squat3–4 × 4–6
  • Bulgarian split squat3 × 6 / leg
  • Single-leg Romanian deadlift3 × 8 / leg
  • Calf raises, heavy (seated + standing)3 × 10–15
  • Plank / side plank3 × 30–45 s

Gym B · Thursday · Power

Explosive, quality over fatigue. Stop each set while you're still springy.

  • Pogo hops3 × 20
  • Box jumps (step down)3 × 5
  • Bounding, from week 53 × 20 m
  • Hip thrust3 × 6
  • Step-ups, weighted3 × 8 / leg
  • Copenhagen plank2 × 20 s / side
  • Tibialis raises2 × 15

Gym C · Friday · Optional

Upper body, core and mobility. 30–40 min, nothing that leaves your legs sore.

  • Pull-ups or lat pulldown3 × 6–10
  • Push-ups or dumbbell press3 × 8–12
  • Dead bug / Pallof press3 × 10
  • Hip and ankle mobility10 min

Rules

  • Weeks 1–4: learn the movements at 2–3 sets of 8–10 with moderate weight. Go heavy from week 5.
  • Lift after the run, ideally 6+ hours later. Gym B goes on the same day as Thursday's quality run, so hard days stay hard and easy days stay easy.
  • Down weeks: cut sets by a third. Taper (weeks 23–24): one session, half the sets, same weights. Race week: no lifting.
  • Heavy strength and plyometric training improves running economy, the oxygen cost of a given pace, without adding much body mass (Balsalobre-Fernández et al., 2016).

SAUNA

Protocol

  • When: straight after easy runs or gym sessions (Mon, Wed, Sat). That's when your core temperature is already up, which is how the studies did it.
  • Heat: 80–90 °C. Time: start at 10–15 min and build to 25–30 min over the first month. Split it (2 × 12–15 min with a cool break) if that's easier.
  • Normal weeks: 3 sessions. Heat blocks (weeks 12–14 before the half, 21–23 before the marathon): 4 sessions, adding Friday.
  • Hydration: weigh yourself before and after. Drink 1.25–1.5 L per kg lost, with some sodium (electrolyte drink or a salty snack).
  • Race weeks: short sessions Monday and Wednesday only. Nothing in the 3 days before a race.
Skip the sauna when: resting HR is 5+ bpm above your normal, you're ill, you've had alcohol, you feel dizzy, or right after a long run (you're already dehydrated). If you have any heart condition, ask your doctor first.

What the evidence says

  • Scoon et al. (2007): 6 male runners did ~30 min of sauna after training for 3 weeks. Time to exhaustion rose about 32% and plasma volume about 7%.
  • Stanley et al. (2015): post-exercise sauna raised plasma volume in trained cyclists within days.
  • Kirby et al. (2021): post-exercise sauna improved markers of exercise capacity in trained middle-distance runners, in both hot and normal conditions.
  • The honest read: these are small studies (6–20 people), and none tested a marathon directly. The mechanism (more plasma volume, better heat tolerance) is solid; the size of the race-day benefit is uncertain. It's a low-cost add-on, not a substitute for running.

FUEL & WEIGHT

Losing weight while training

  • Deficit on easy days only: about 300–500 kcal under maintenance. Eat fully on quality and long-run days, so the sessions that make you faster aren't underfuelled.
  • Rate: about 0.5–1% of body weight a week (roughly 1–2 lb now). Faster than that costs training quality and muscle.
  • Protein: about 1.6–2.2 g per kg per day, spread across meals (Morton et al., 2018).
  • No deficit in the taper (weeks 23–25) or race week.
  • Lighter means less weight to carry each stride, so running economy improves as you lose it. This is your biggest lever toward a faster time.

Race fuelling

  • Long runs over 90 min: start at 30–60 g carbs per hour and train your gut up to 60–90 g/h by week 21 (Jeukendrup, 2014). Use the gels or drink you'll race with.
  • Carb-load: 10–12 g carbs per kg per day for the 36–48 hours before the race (Burke et al., 2011). Low fibre, familiar foods.
  • Race morning: 1–4 g carbs per kg, 1–4 hours before the start.
  • Race: 60–90 g/h from early on, plus fluid to thirst.

SCIENCE

Why the plan looks like this

  • Mostly easy: about 80% of running is easy, below your HR cap, and about 20% is hard. Elite endurance athletes train this way (Seiler, 2010), and world-class distance runners use a mostly-easy, pyramid-shaped mix (Casado et al., 2022).
  • Gradual build: mileage rises in small steps with a down week every 3–4 weeks. Runners who increased volume by more than 30% over two weeks were more likely to get injured (Nielsen et al., 2014).
  • Paces from fitness, not from the goal: each pace comes from your current VDOT (Daniels' model), and the checkpoints update it. Training at goal pace before you're fit enough mostly adds injury risk.
  • Marathon specificity late: threshold and VO2 work build the engine in weeks 5–14; marathon-pace runs and long runs up to 30 km come in weeks 17–22.
  • Taper: volume drops about 20%, 40% and then 65% over the last three weeks while some intensity stays. That's the pattern that improved performance most in a meta-analysis (Bosquet et al., 2007).
